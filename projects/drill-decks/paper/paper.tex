\begin{abstract}
Two practice decks are built from the player's own games. The misses deck files each position
where he went wrong under a theme word, which orders a session and, for most words, names the
skill a solve counts toward. Reading each of its \Scored{} engine-checked positions by what its
own stored lines show, \DefensiveShare\% are clear defensive misses (he had to stop something)
and \AttackingShare\% clear missed wins. The deck files \PhaseShare\% of its positions under a
phase name, among them \PhaseDefensive{} clear defensive misses and \PhaseAttacking{} clear
missed wins, and only \DefRecall\% of the clear defensive misses carry its defensive-move word.
The guess-the-plan quiz has \Quizzes{} positions; by the app's own rule \TwoRight{} of them have
two right answers out of three, the best option is a capture, a check or a two-pawn lead in
\ForcingOrMaterial, and \PlanQuestions{} ask a plan question with one right answer. A search with
the move passed to his opponent finds a threat in \ThreatShare\% of the positions: \NeitherThreatShare\%
of those whose lines show no material change, while \SelfShare\% of the clear defensive misses had no
threat to answer, so his own move made the loss. The deck's \Quiet{} quiet positions hold
\QuietQuiz{} plan questions with one right answer.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question}

The misses deck is the player's own mistakes as practice. Each position carries a theme word
from the app's labeller of the best move (\texttt{themeOfBest}): a motif when one of its detectors
fires (a mate in one, a back-rank mate, a fork, a raid on f7, a free piece, a defensive move),
else the phase. The word interleaves a session, and for the words the learner knows it is the
skill a solve or a miss is counted toward. The guess-the-plan quiz shows a position from his
games with three engine moves and asks which is the plan; an option within \Margin{} centipawns
of the best counts as right. The detector-themes project scored the labeller on puzzles, which
are tactics by construction. Here it is read on the deck itself (app commit
\texttt{\AppCommit}), against the deck's own engine lines.

\section{Method}

The rules were written in the project's README before any of these numbers was computed. What
the best move did is read from its stored line (the routine's deep pass, five lines at four
million nodes) by the board code alone: it \emph{mates} (the line ends in mate or scores a
mate), \emph{wins material} (his balance, counting 1, 3, 3, 5, 9, is at least two points up at
the line's end, the lower of the last two balances when it ends on a capture), or neither. What
his move cost is read the same way from its own stored line, or from the move and the deck's
punishing line: it \emph{allowed mate}, \emph{lost material} (two points or more), or neither.
A miss is \emph{defensive} when the best move wins nothing and his move allowed mate or lost
material, \emph{attacking} when the best move mates or wins and his move lost nothing, and
\emph{mixed} otherwise: \Both{} where both happened and \Neither{} (\NeitherShare\%) where the
stored lines show no material change either way, so material cannot judge them. The
\Deviations{} opening deviations, which have no engine lines, are left out.

By the app's rule, \OneRight{} quizzes have one right answer and \TwoRight{} (\TwoRightShare\%)

with one right answer. In the position above (\QuizFen), the best option is \QuizLine{} (\QuizUci).

\section{What his opponent threatened}

The stored lines say what the best move and his move did, not whether he had anything to answer.
So each scored position was searched by the app's engine at the routine's punishing budget
(\Budget{} nodes), as he faced it and with the move passed to his opponent, \Searches{} searches in
all; the \InCheck{} positions where he was in check cannot be passed and are counted apart. By the
rules the README fixed before the search ran, a position is \emph{threatened} when the opponent's
line after the pass mates or wins him two points or more, counted as the census counts a gain.
\Threatened{} of the \Searched{} passed positions (\ThreatShare\%) were: \ThreatMate{} by mate and
\ThreatMaterial{} by material. Passing cost him a median \PassThreat{} points of win\% in those and
\PassNot{} in the rest, where it is the value of the move itself.

\paragraph{A third of the positions with no material change were defences.} Of the
\NeitherSearched{} positions whose lines show no material change, \NeitherThreat{}
(\NeitherThreatShare\%) were threatened: defensive misses the stored lines could not show. The other
\Quiet{} are quiet: nothing was threatened, and nothing was won or lost.

\paragraph{Two in five clear defensive misses had no threat to answer.} Of the \DefSearched{}
clear defensive misses searched, \DefThreat{} (\DefThreatShare\%) were threatened. In the other
\SelfInflicted{} (\SelfShare\%) nothing was threatened before he moved: his move made the loss. Those
ask what his own move allows, not what his opponent's move threatens.

\paragraph{The defensive-move word is right more often than the lines show.} The gate fires on
every defensive-move position, since the labeller's defensive move protects a piece the gate reads as
loose. The search finds a threat in \DefWordThreat{} of its \DefWordSearched{} searched positions
(\DefWordThreatShare\%), where the stored lines alone found a clear defensive miss in \DefFit\%. In
\DefWordPawnOnly{} of the other \DefWordFalse{}, the loose piece was a pawn alone. The word is on
\DefWordOfThreats\% of all the threatened positions; phase words carry \PhaseThreat{}
(\PhaseThreatOfThreats\%).

\paragraph{The gate misses the threats the phase words hide.} Over all the searched positions the
gate's precision is \GatePrecision\% and its recall \GateRecall\%: it misses \GateMissed{} threats.
Of its \GateFalse{} false alarms, \GatePawnOnly{} (\GatePawnOnlyShare\%) are a loose pawn alone,
which the two-point rule never counts. On the \PhaseThreat{} threatened positions filed under a
phase, it fires on \PhaseGateRecall\%. The rest held a threat of another kind than the three it reads
(a loose piece, the f7 strike, a mate in one), and the labeller, which reads the same loose piece,
could not call them defensive.

\section{What it means for the app}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item The misses deck's word should come from what the position shows before the detectors are
asked. One more search per miss, with the move passed, at the budget the routine already uses
to punish, tells the three kinds apart: a threat he had to answer, a loss his own move made, and a
quiet position.
\item A phase word should not feed a skill: the endgame positions that were defensive misses
should not count toward conversion.
\item The quiz should be drawn from positions where one option is right and the best option is
quiet, or say when a position is a calculation question. The misses deck's quiet positions supply
\QuietQuiz{} such positions.
\item The live coach's instant gate misses \GateMissed{} of the \Threatened{} threats the search
finds, most of them filed under a phase, and a loose pawn is most of what it flags that the search
does not count.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limits}

The rule reads material only. A capture that removes an attacker and also wins material reads as
a missed win: \DefWinsSmall{} of the \DefWins{} defensive-move positions read as wins gain
exactly two points, and some of those are such captures, where the word is right. The threat
search reads what the opponent would do with a free move, which is not a fair question where
moving at all hurts him, and it counts a threat that his best move happened to stop; the two-point
rule leaves out a pawn. The lines are the engine's, at the budgets the routine uses.
