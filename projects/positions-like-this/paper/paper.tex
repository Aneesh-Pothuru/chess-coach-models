\title{Can the app find strong players' positions like his, whose move works in his?\\[0.3em]
\large Matches by pawns, pieces and kings from \IndexGames{} strong players' games, against
random matches with the same pieces, refereed by Stockfish}
\author{Chess Coach research lab}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Can the app find positions from strong players' games so like a position from his games that
the strong player's move works in his too? For \ScoredAll{} quiet middlegame and endgame positions
from his \Games{} 10-minute games, an index of \IndexPositions{} positions from \IndexGames{}
Lichess games between players rated \Floor{} or more gives the \Arm{} nearest positions with the
same pieces, by a distance over the pawns, the pieces and the kings, and \Arm{} random positions
with the same pieces, and Stockfish says which of the strong players' moves are sound in his
position. By the rule fixed before any move was scored, likeness works: the nearest positions'
moves are sound \TFirst\% and \TSecond\% of the time on the two halves of his games, against
\RFirst\% and \RSecond\% for random ones, a lift of \LiftFirst{} and \LiftSecond{} points with
both intervals above 0, and both controls pass. It works too weakly to give advice: his own move
is sound \AFirst\% and \ASecond\% of the time, and Maia-2's most likely move at his band \NFirst\%
and \NSecond\%. A match's move is sound more often the nearer its position, from \DistTenFirst\%
and \DistTenSecond\% at a distance of 10 or more to \DistZeroFirst\% and \DistZeroSecond\% within
2, but most of his positions have no match nearer than 10, and the few with one within 2 do no
better there than Maia-2's move. Added after the run: \ScaleFactor{} times as many games moves the
median distance of his nearest match only from \ScaleMedianFirst{} to \ScaleMedianLast. The app
takes no search for similar positions from this. The lift is largest where the app names the
pawn structure, so what carries from a strong player's game to his is more likely the plan the
structure calls for than the next move.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question}

A coach explains a middlegame or endgame decision with an example: this is like a game where a
strong player had the same pawns and pieces and played this move. The example teaches a plan
only when the positions are alike in the way that matters, so that the strong player's move works
in the student's position too. The app keys pawn skeletons exactly and has no search for similar
positions. This study asks whether one would find such examples for him. For a quiet middlegame
or endgame position from his games, it finds the positions from games between strong players
nearest to his by a distance over the pawns, the pieces and the kings, and asks whether the strong
players' moves there are sound in his position more often than the moves of strong players from
random positions with the same pieces.

If likeness works, a ``this is like\ldots'' example in the review or a drill can show a strong
player's position like his with the move played there, and the likeness is part of why the move
works. If a random position with the same pieces does as well, the likeness an example claims is
not why its move works, and the app has no reason to search for one.

\section{How it was measured}

\paragraph{His positions.} His \Games{} 10-minute games in the app's archive at commit
\texttt{\AppCommit}, in order of their end time: the first \GamesFirst{} (\FirstFrom{} to
\FirstTo) and the last \GamesSecond{} (\SecondFrom{} to \SecondTo) are the two halves. Nothing is
fitted, so the halves are two replications. Each of his moves enters when the position is past
the opening by the app's divider, he is not in check and has at least two legal moves, the board
holds \MinMen{} men or more (fewer is the tablebase's ground), his win\% before the move is
between \WinLow{} and \WinHigh{} by the archive's evaluation, and his clock shows at least
\ClockFloor{} seconds. That leaves \Candidates{} positions, \CandFirst{} in the first half and
\CandSecond{} in the second; the referee below keeps the quiet ones.

\paragraph{Strong players' positions.} Lichess's \MonthName{} database (CC0) gives the first
\Cap{} rated blitz, rapid and classical games in file order between two players rated \Floor{} or
more that ended normally or on time and have at least \MinPlies{} plies, out of \GamesRead{}
games read. Most are blitz: \Blitz{} blitz, \Rapid{} rapid and \Classical{} classical. One game in
\HeldOutOneIn, by a seeded hash of its id, is held out (\HeldOutGames{} games); the other
\IndexGames{} are the index. Every position before a move in the index games that is past the
opening by the app's divider, not in check, holds \MinMen{} men or more, and where the mover's
clock shows at least \ClockFloor{} seconds enters the index with the move played there:
\IndexPositions{} positions.

\paragraph{Alike.} Every position is read from the side to move: when Black is to move, the board
is mirrored top to bottom and the colours swapped, so the mover always plays up the board, and
moves are mirrored the same way. Two positions can match only when each side has the same number
of queens, rooks, knights and pawns, and of bishops on each colour of square. The
\emph{distance} between two such positions adds three parts:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{pawns}: the squares where one position has a pawn of a side and the other does not,
  so a pawn one square further on counts 2;
\item \textbf{pieces}: for each side and each kind of piece (knight, bishop on each colour, rook,
  queen), the two positions' pieces paired to make the total least, each pair counting the
  king-step distance between its squares, at most \PieceCap;
\item \textbf{kings}: each king's king-step distance between the two positions, at most \KingCap.
\end{itemize}
The weights were set without looking at any position: the pawns, which fix the plans, count the
most, and a piece counts at most a moved pawn. A match \emph{transfers} when its move, read in his
position, is legal there and moves the same kind of piece from the same square.

\paragraph{The moves compared.} In each position, \textbf{T} is the strong players' moves from
the \Arm{} nearest index positions with the same pieces, from \Arm{} different games, whose moves
transfer, ties in distance broken by a seeded hash. \textbf{R} is the moves from \Arm{} index
positions with the same pieces drawn at random, from \Arm{} more games, none of them T's, whose
moves transfer, after at most \RandomDraws{} draws. \textbf{R2} is a second random draw the same
way, from games none of T's or R's. His positions also have \textbf{N}, Maia-2's most likely move
at his band; \textbf{U}, its most likely move at the band \Above{} points above; and \textbf{A},
his move. His band is his rating at the game with the app's offset of \Offset{} points, the band
above the same of his rating plus \Above, each conditioned on the opponent's band. A position is
\emph{covered} when T, R and R2 each find \Arm{} moves.

\paragraph{The referee.} Stockfish through the app's engine service at the grading budget of
\GradeNodes{} nodes, with its hash cleared before each position and no tablebase, gives the best
move and scores every move compared. A move is \emph{sound} when it gives up no more than
\Window{} win\% against the best, by the app's own test for an alternative move. A position is
\emph{quiet} when the best move is not a capture, a check or a promotion and the win\% it gives
the side to move is between \WinLow{} and \WinHigh; only quiet positions are scored. A score
that is not exact counts as missing, and a position with a missing score leaves the analysis.

\paragraph{The measures.} In each position, an arm's \emph{soundness} is the share of its \Arm{}
moves that are sound. The \emph{lift} is T's soundness minus R's, averaged over positions, paired
by position. \Draws{} bootstrap draws resample his games within each half, or the strong players'
positions; intervals are the 95\% percentile intervals.

\paragraph{The rule, fixed before any move was scored.} Two controls come first, and if either
fails the study gives no verdict.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{The planted twin.} \StrongQueries{} held-out strong players' positions, one a game,
  covered and quiet by the same filters (a game tries another of its positions, at most \Tries,
  when one is not). Planted in the index with its own move, each would be its own nearest match,
  at distance 0, with a move that transfers. The twin's move must be sound more often than R's
  moves: the interval of the twin's soundness minus R's lies above 0.
\item \textbf{The null.} R2 against R, in place of T against R, must read neither as likeness
  working nor as likeness misleading.
\end{enumerate}
Then, on his positions, \emph{likeness works} when the lift's interval lies above 0 on both
halves, \emph{likeness misleads} when it lies below 0 on both, and it is \emph{no better than a
random match} otherwise. A half with fewer than \MinPositions{} scored positions is \emph{not
enough data}, whatever the numbers say. The rule and the scripts went to the lab before any move
was scored; the coverage of the final index was counted after the rule and before any move was
scored.

\section{Coverage}

\CoverageTable

The index covers most of his positions but rarely closely (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). It finds
\Arm{} matches for each arm in \CoveredFirst\% of his candidate positions in the first half and
\CoveredSecond\% in the second, and in \StrongCovered\% of a sample of \StrongSample{} held-out
strong players' positions. The nearest match that transfers has a median distance of
\NearMedianFirst{} and \NearMedianSecond{} in the two halves, and \StrongNearMedian{} for the
strong players' positions: a typical match differs from his position in several pawns and most
of the pieces.

Of the covered positions, the referee keeps the quiet ones: \ScoredFirst{} positions from
\ScoredGamesFirst{} games in the first half and \ScoredSecond{} from \ScoredGamesSecond{} games in
the second. In \ForcingFirst{} and \ForcingSecond{} positions the best move is a capture, a check
or a promotion, and in \DecidedFirst{} and \DecidedSecond{} the referee reads the game as decided.
Every score was exact.

\section{The controls}

\paragraph{The planted twin passes.} In \StrongPositions{} held-out strong players' positions, the
strong player's own move is sound \TwinSound\% of the time (\TwinSoundCi) and a random match's
move \TwinR\% (\TwinRCi); the difference, \TwinLift{} points (\TwinLiftCi), lies far above 0. The
referee tells a move that belongs to a position from one that only fits it. A strong player's own
move is sound in only about two thirds of their positions: most of the index is blitz, and a
strong player's blitz move is often more than \Window{} win\% from Stockfish's best. That is no
ceiling for a match: the nearest matches of strong players' positions are sound \StrongDistZero\%
of the time (Table~\ref{tab:distance}), since a move that players chose in nearly the same
position is sound more often than one player's move in one game.

\paragraph{The null passes.} R2 against R reads \NullFirst{} points (\NullFirstCi) in the first
half and \NullSecond{} points (\NullSecondCi) in the second; both intervals hold 0.

\section{The verdict: likeness works}

\ArmTable

The strong players' moves from the \Arm{} nearest positions are sound in his position \TFirst\%
and \TSecond\% of the time on the two halves, and the random matches' \RFirst\% and \RSecond\%
(Table~\ref{tab:arms}). The lift is \LiftFirst{} points (\LiftFirstCi) and \LiftSecond{} points
(\LiftSecondCi), above 0 on both halves, so by the rule likeness works. A second random draw does
no better than the first: R2's moves are sound \RTwoFirst\% and \RTwoSecond\% of the time.

It works far too weakly to give advice. His own move is sound \AFirst\% and \ASecond\% of the
time, Maia-2's most likely move at his band \NFirst\% and \NSecond\%, and at the band above
\UFirst\% and \USecond\%: a strong player's move from a position like his is sound about half as
often as his own. On average the matches' moves give up \LostTFirst{} and \LostTSecond{} win\%
against the best, about one point less than the random matches' (\LostRFirst{} and \LostRSecond),
while his own moves give up \LostAFirst{} and \LostASecond{} (Table~\ref{tab:more}). The
matches' moves are the referee's best \BestTFirst\% and \BestTSecond\% of the time, the random
matches' \BestRFirst\% and \BestRSecond\%.

\section{What the positions describe}

\DistanceTable

\paragraph{Nearer is better.} A match's move is sound more often the nearer its position
(Table~\ref{tab:distance}): \DistZeroFirst\% and \DistZeroSecond\% of the matches within 2 on the
two halves, \DistThreeFirst\% and \DistThreeSecond\% from 3 to 5, \DistSixFirst\% and
\DistSixSecond\% from 6 to 9, and \DistTenFirst\% and \DistTenSecond\% at 10 or more. Strong
players' own positions show the same, from \StrongDistZero\% within 2 to \StrongDistTen\% at 10
or more. Almost every match within 9 is one of T's; at 10 or more, where all of R's are, a little
under half are T's.

\SplitTable

\paragraph{Middlegame and structure.} The lift is larger in the middlegame, \MidLiftFirst{} and
\MidLiftSecond{} points, than in the endgame, \EndLiftFirst{} and \EndLiftSecond{} points, where
the second half's interval holds 0 (Table~\ref{tab:splits}). It is larger where the app names the
position's pawn structure, \NamedLiftFirst{} and \NamedLiftSecond{} points, than where it names
none, \NoneLiftFirst{} and \NoneLiftSecond. In the strong players' own positions the lift is
\StrongLift{} points (\StrongLiftCi).

\MoreTable

\paragraph{New to him, and his mistakes.} A sound move he rarely finds, one that is not his move
and that Maia-2 gives under \Hard\% at his band, comes from T in \NewTFirst\% and \NewTSecond\% of
his positions and from R in \NewRFirst\% and \NewRSecond\%: likeness adds no idea he would miss.
Where his own move is not sound, in \WrongFirst{} and \WrongSecond{} positions, at least one of
T's moves is sound in \WrongTFirst\% and \WrongTSecond\% of them, and one of R's in \WrongRFirst\%
and \WrongRSecond\%.

\section{Added after the run}

These descriptions were added once the rule had been applied; none was named before the run.

\NearestTable

\paragraph{Only a near match competes.} By the distance of each position's nearest match
(Table~\ref{tab:nearest}), \FarShare\% of his positions have none nearer than 10, and there the
nearest match's move is sound \FarSound\% of the time, against \FarA\% for his own move and
\FarMaia\% for Maia-2's most likely move at his band. Only \NearTwoShare\% of his positions,
\NearTwoCount{} in all, have a match within 2. There its move is sound \NearTwoSound\% of the
time, about as often as his own move (\NearTwoA\%) and no more often than Maia-2's
(\NearTwoMaia\%). These are easier positions: his own move and Maia-2's are sound more often there
too.

\ScaleTable

\paragraph{A bigger index helps slowly.} Restricting the index to the first games of the stream
(Table~\ref{tab:scale}), \ScaleFactor{} times as many games moves the median distance of his
positions' nearest match from \ScaleMedianFirst{} to \ScaleMedianLast, about one for each
doubling, and the share with a match within 2 from \ScaleTwoFirst\% to \ScaleTwoLast\%. Near
matches grow with the index, but slowly.

\section{What the app can take}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{No search for similar positions.} Likeness works by the rule, but a strong player's
  move from a position like his is sound about half as often as his own, and the near matches,
  the only ones that do as well as Maia-2's most likely move at his band, are rare. A ``this is
  like\ldots'' example built this way would mostly show a move that does not work in his
  position.
\item \textbf{An example is checked first.} If the app ever shows a strong player's position as an
  example, the engine checks its move in his position before it is shown, and only a near match
  is worth showing.
\item \textbf{A plan, not a move.} The lift is largest where the app names the pawn structure.
  What carries from a strong player's game to his is more likely the plan the structure calls
  for than the next move. A next round can match on the structure and test a plan over the next
  few moves, with its rule fixed first.
\end{itemize}

\section{Caveats}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{A move stands for a plan.} The strong player's next move is tested, not the moves
  that follow it.
\item \textbf{The board alone.} The study's plan also named Maia-2's reading of the position as
  part of alike. The app's Maia-2 gives move probabilities and no reading of who is better, so
  this round matches on the board alone.
\item \textbf{Sound is a window.} In a quiet position several moves are often within \Window{}
  win\% of the best, so the arms are paired by position. A strong player's own move clears the
  window about two times in three.
\item \textbf{Mostly blitz.} Most index games are blitz; his games are rapid.
\item \textbf{The same month.} The held-out strong positions come from the same month as the
  index, so the same players can be on both sides; their result describes and decides nothing.
\item \textbf{A coverage count ran first.} Before the rule was fixed, a partial index from the
  first games of the stream showed that his positions' nearest matches were far, and the index
  was grown to \Cap{} games before any move was scored.
\item \textbf{Added after the run.} The grouping by the nearest match's distance, the index-size
  curve and the two positions were looked at once the rule had been applied. They describe; a
  rule built on them needs positions this study did not see.
\end{itemize}
