\title{Does the learner know how often he solves a puzzle?\\[0.3em]
\large The coaching app's solve chances, replayed over the player's puzzle record}
\author{Chess Coach research lab}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The coaching app sets the difficulty of each puzzle set from a Glicko-2 rating: it aims where its
learner says the player will solve \TargetCore\% of the time. Replayed over the profile as the app
held it when this study began (\PAttempts{} puzzle attempts on \PDays{} days, logged as he
played), the rating serving then read gave him a mean solve chance of \PHead\% on puzzles he
solved \PSolved\% of. It sat close to the difficulty he was served, so the sets aimed far below
him. The learner's view of his themed sets, which it already kept, gave \PView\%, still low, with
about half the log loss. The test committed before that run used a record rebuilt from the app's
memory cards, which turned out to keep almost only his solves, because a missed puzzle comes back
for another review; its verdicts do not stand. The app's owner adopted the view on the profile's
look. A second round, fixed before any of its attempts existed, then put both estimates to the
\TwoAttempts{} first exposures the app recorded next, \TwoMisses{} of them misses. By its rules
the headline rating under-predicts him (a mean chance of \TwoHead\% against \TwoSolved\% solved),
and the view, at \TwoView\%, does not pass: its log loss is lower on both halves of the days, but
on the second the interval of the difference reaches above zero. Whether the app goes back to the
headline is its owner's call, and he kept the view while the testing goes on. A third round, the
last, puts the same rule to twice the days, and a test of the chance by puzzle depth waits for its
attempts.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question}

The app's learner keeps a Glicko-2 rating for each skill and turns it into the chance that the
player solves an item of a given difficulty: Glicko's expected score against the item's rating,
with the item's own deviation. Serving reads that chance backwards. A bucket's puzzle set aims at
the difficulty he should solve \TargetCore\% of the time (\TargetNew\% while the bucket is new),
and draws the unseen puzzles nearest to it. The profile's puzzle totals count \TotalCorrect{}
solved of \TotalAttempts.

The learner keeps two estimates of each skill. The \emph{headline rating} folds every channel and
counts a result in an announced set at the Lichess discount, so that a record earned in announced
sets does not overstate how he plays when nothing is announced: in a bucket's own set a solve moves
it \ThemedWin{} as far as one in an unannounced set and a miss \ThemedLoss{} as far, and for an
obvious theme such as mate in N \ObviousWin{} and \ObviousLoss. The legacy attempt log cannot say
whether an attempt was a first try, so each of its attempts counts at \UnknownFirst{} of that. Beside it, a
\emph{view} per channel folds only that channel's first exposures, each at full weight. When this
study began, serving read the headline. The learner's own notes expect the discount to read a
player served at \TargetCore\% in themed sets about \NotesPoints{} points under his strength. When
the learner gives a solve chance, how often does he solve? And is the rating serving read the
right one?

\section{The record and the rule}

\paragraph{The record.} The rule was committed before any solve chance was computed. It named a
record rebuilt so that every first try has its time. The app keeps a memory card for every puzzle
he has been served, with its last review's time and its passes and fails, and the bank gives each
puzzle's bucket and Lichess rating. A card reviewed exactly once holds one first try with its time
and result; a card reviewed more than once keeps only its last review's time, so it was left out.
That leaves \Once{} of the \Cards{} cards: \FromEvents{} with the app's own attempt event and
\Rebuilt{} rebuilt as the event the app writes today, \RFirstTries{} first tries from \RFrom{} to
\RTo. The cards, like everything else round 1 reads, come from the profile at commit
\ProfileCommit, the one round 1 measured; what the app records after it is round 2's.

\paragraph{The instrument.} For every first try, in time order, the app's own learner at commit
\AppCommit{} is folded on the record before it, at the moment of the attempt, and gives two solve
chances for the puzzle: from the headline rating and from the view of the attempt's channel (for a
bucket's own set, the themed view). An attempt is scored when its skill already has a rated attempt
in that channel, so both exist. A solve scores $-\ln p$ and a miss $-\ln(1-p)$, as the learner's
own self-check scores them.

\paragraph{The candidate.} Read a set's solve chance, and so its target, from the view of the set's
own channel, falling back to the headline where the view has no attempt yet. It fits nothing.

\paragraph{The rule.} The days with scored attempts alternate between two halves. (1) The headline
under-predicts if the share solved minus its mean chance has a 95\% interval over \Draws{}
bootstrap draws of the days (seed \Seed) entirely above zero, over-predicts if entirely below, and
is calibrated within the noise otherwise. (2) The view replaces the headline only if, on each
half, its mean log loss is below the headline's with the interval of the difference entirely below
zero. Named beside the verdicts, deciding nothing: the same replay on the profile as the app holds
it, scores against a running rate (the share of the skill's earlier attempts he solved), a
reliability table, the buckets, a best-fitting shift, the learner's self-check, and where each
estimate would aim a set today.

\section{Round 1, and why its verdicts do not stand}

On the rebuilt record (\RAttempts{} scored attempts on \RDays{} days) he solved \RSolved\%, and the
headline's mean chance was \RHead\%: a gap of \RGap{} points (\RGapLo{} to \RGapHi), so by rule 1
the headline under-predicts. By rule 2 the view passes on both halves: its log loss minus the
headline's is \HOneDiff{} on the first (\HOneDays{} days, \HOneLo{} to \HOneHi) and \HTwoDiff{} on
the second (\HTwoDays{} days, \HTwoLo{} to \HTwoHi).

Neither verdict stands. A missed puzzle becomes a redemption card that comes back until he solves
it, while a solved one retires, so a card reviewed once is nearly always a solve
(Table~\ref{tab:cards}). Leaving out the cards reviewed more than once removed \MoreWithMiss{} of
the \AllWithMiss{} puzzles he ever missed, which is the outcome both rules measure. The rebuilt
record's \RSolved\% is that selection, and on a record with almost no misses a higher chance can
only score better, so rule 2 could hardly fail. The memory a scheduler keeps is shaped by the results
it reacts to; a record of first tries has to come from a log written as they were played.

\section{The profile as the app held it}

The profile round 1 read also holds what the app logged as he played: the legacy attempt log (\LogEntries{}
entries, \LogOnce{} of them on cards reviewed once and \LogMore{} on the others, all agreeing with
the cards in time, bucket, rating and result), the puzzle totals from before the log, which the
learner folds as a seed, and the attempt events. Every attempt in it was logged when it was played,
so nothing in it selects on the result. Replayed the same way, it gives \PAttempts{} scored attempts
on \PDays{} days, from \PFrom{} to \PTo, with \PMisses{} misses: he solved \PSolved\%.

The headline's mean chance was \PHead\%, a gap of \PGap{} points (\PGapLo{} to \PGapHi); the
view's was \PView\%, a gap of \PViewGap{} (\PViewGapLo{} to \PViewGapHi). Both under-predict, the
headline by far more (Table~\ref{tab:scores}). The view's log loss is about half the headline's,
a difference of \PDiff{} (\PDiffLo{} to \PDiffHi), and the reliability table shows where: the headline put
most of his attempts below a 70\% chance, and he solved nearly all of them
(Table~\ref{tab:reliability}). The same holds in every bucket with more than one attempt
(Table~\ref{tab:buckets}). The view scores only about as well as the running rate, though: the share of the skill's earlier
attempts he solved.

Why the headline reads him so low shows in its own fold. It starts each skill at the median rating
of the puzzles he was served, and a solve in a mate set that the log cannot call a first try moves
it \ObviousUnknownWin{} of a full step. So it follows the puzzles up and never gets ahead of them:
on the profile it sat on average \HeadAbove{} points above the puzzles he was asked about (their
mean rating \ItemMean), where Glicko gives a little over half, while the view sat \ViewAbove{}
points above. Figure~\ref{fig:mate} shows it on mate in one, where he solved \MateSolved{} of
\MateN. The shift that best fits the headline's chances, taken off every puzzle's rating, is
\PShiftHead; the view's is \PShiftView.

\section{What round 1 meant for the app}

The sets aimed far below him. On the profile round 1 read, the view aimed a set \UpMin{} to
\UpMax{} points higher than the headline in the \WithView{} buckets that had a themed view
(Table~\ref{tab:today}). The other \WithoutView{} had no attempt in their own sets since the log
began, and the app came to aim such a bucket from its stored totals read alone at full weight,
there \TotUpMin{} to \TotUpMax{} points higher than the headline. The bank could serve every one of
those targets. The view still under-predicted him, so sets aimed from it should still land above
\TargetCore\%, but nearer.

By its rule round 1 adopted nothing: its test did not stand, and the profile's look, named beside
it, decides nothing. On that look the app's owner chose to adopt the view at once: since chess PR
78 the app aims a bucket's set from its themed view, and the skill map's next difficulty reads the
same estimate; since PR 79, a bucket with no themed view aims from its stored totals instead of the
headline. The commit round 1's replay runs on, \AppCommit, has both. Round 2 was left to decide
whether the view stays.

\section{Round 2}

\paragraph{The attempts.} Round 2 was fixed before any of its attempts existed. It takes every
attempt in a bucket's own set that the app counts as a first exposure, recorded after the last
attempt in the profile round 1 read (played at \TwoCutoffClock{} on \LastDay, Los Angeles time),
each folded on everything before it as the app holds it, and it runs once those attempts fall on
at least ten days. Rules 1 and 2 apply unchanged: the app by then aimed its sets from the view,
and each estimate is still scored on the puzzles he was served. Its script was committed before it
ran, and rules 1 and 2 run once: on the profile at app commit \TwoCommit{} (synced on \TwoSynced)
with that commit's own learner. It found \TwoAll{} such attempts on \TwoAllDays{} days, from \TwoFrom{} to \TwoTo;
another \TwoLeftOut{} were on a puzzle he had seen before and are left out. Of the \TwoAll,
\TwoAttempts{} are scored, on \TwoDays{} days; the other \TwoOpened{} opened a bucket's record in
its own sets, where no view exists yet.

He solved \TwoSolved\% of the scored attempts and missed \TwoMisses, where the profile round 1
read holds \PMisses{} misses. That share lies between the \TargetCore\% the app aims at and the
\TargetNew\% it aims at while a bucket is new. On the profile round 1 read, whose legacy log could
not tell a first try from a retry, he had solved \PSolved\%.

\paragraph{Rule 1.} The headline's mean chance on the scored attempts was \TwoHead\%, a gap of
\TwoGap{} points to the share he solved (95\% interval \TwoGapLo{} to \TwoGapHi). By rule 1 the
headline under-predicts him.

\paragraph{Rule 2.} The scored days alternate between two halves in time order. On the first
(\TwoHOneDays{} days, \TwoHOneAttempts{} attempts) the view's mean log loss was \TwoHOneView{}
against the headline's \TwoHOneHead, a difference of \TwoHOneDiff{} (\TwoHOneLo{} to \TwoHOneHi).
On the second (\TwoHTwoDays{} days, \TwoHTwoAttempts{} attempts) it was \TwoHTwoView{} against
\TwoHTwoHead, a difference of \TwoHTwoDiff{} (\TwoHTwoLo{} to \TwoHTwoHi). The view scores better
on both halves, but the rule needs both intervals entirely below zero, and the second half's
reaches above it. By rule 2 the view does not pass. The second half is the thin one: its days hold
\TwoHTwoList{} scored attempts, and the first half's hold \TwoHOneList.

\paragraph{Beside the verdicts, deciding nothing.} The view's mean chance was \TwoView\%, a gap of
\TwoViewGap{} points (\TwoViewGapLo{} to \TwoViewGapHi): it still under-predicts him, by about a
quarter of the headline's gap. Over all \TwoDays{} days its log loss minus the headline's is
\TwoDiff{} (\TwoDiffLo{} to \TwoDiffHi), and it scores best of the three estimates on both scores
(Table~\ref{tab:scores2}). The shift that best fits the headline's chances takes \TwoShiftHead{}
off every puzzle's rating; the view's takes \TwoShiftView.

His share solved hardly moves with either estimate's chance (Table~\ref{tab:reliability2}): over
the bins that hold at least ten attempts it runs from \TwoBinLo\% to \TwoBinHi\%, while the bins'
mean chances run from \TwoBinChanceLo\% to \TwoBinChanceHi\%. What separates the two estimates on
these attempts is their level, more than which puzzles each calls easy. By bucket
(Table~\ref{tab:buckets2}) the view's chance is the nearer to his share solved in \TwoViewNearer{}
of the \TwoBuckets; the headline's is nearer in \TwoHeadNearerList. The learner's self-check on
this profile predicted \TwoSelfPred\% on its \TwoSelfN{} held-out attempts, of which he solved
\TwoSelfObs\%; it beats its constant on log loss (\TwoSelfLL{} against \TwoSelfBaseLL) and not on
the Brier score (\TwoSelfBrier{} against \TwoSelfBaseBrier).

\paragraph{Rule 3.} A third rule was fixed after round 2's first attempts were recorded and before
any of their results was read. The app aims every puzzle of a set at one target, whatever the
puzzle's depth, and a target per depth, one for one-move puzzles and a lower one for longer
ones, would land where it says only if the solve chance is as good at one depth as at the other.
Rule 3 tests that: the share solved minus the mean chance on one-move puzzles, minus the same on
puzzles of two moves or more, with its interval over the same draws of the days. It needs
\TwoNeeded{} scored attempts at each depth, and the profile at \TwoRuleThreeCommit{} holds
\TwoOneScored{} of one move and \TwoTwoScored{} of two or more. So it has not run, and nothing of
the attempts' results by depth has been read. It runs once, at a later check, on every attempt
recorded by then, with the chance from the headline, the estimate rule 2 leaves serving, and it
reports the same statistic on the view's chance beside. The project's README states it in full.

\section{What it means for the app now}

By rule 2 the view does not stay: a set's solve chance, and so its target, goes back to the
headline. Rule 1, on the same attempts, finds the headline \TwoGap{} points under his results, and
over all the days the view scores better beyond the noise. On the profile round 2 read the app
aims every bucket that has a view from it, and going back would aim those \TwoWithView{} buckets
\TwoDropMin{} to \TwoDropMax{} points lower, \TwoDropMedian{} at the median
(Table~\ref{tab:today2}). Round 2 says nothing of the stored totals a bucket without a view aims
from: it scored no attempt there.

The rule was written to keep an estimate from being adopted on noise, and a half of \TwoHTwoDays{}
days that falls short is what it refuses; the lab reports the verdict as the rule gives it. What
the app does with it is its owner's call. He was asked on the day round 2 ran, and he chose to
keep the view and go on testing: the app is unchanged, and the next verdict is round 3's.

That third round was fixed on the day round 2 ran, before any of its attempts was read. It puts rules 1 and 2,
unchanged, to the first exposures recorded after the last attempt round 2 read, once they fall on
at least twenty days, twice round 2's. Its rule 2 verdict replaces round 2's. A second test of one
candidate raises the chance that it passes by luck, so both rounds' verdicts are reported side by
side, and round 3 is the last: no later round puts this candidate to the same rule.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item One player. The profile round 1 read holds \PMisses{} misses, so its log losses rest on a
handful of them; round 2's attempts hold \TwoMisses.
\item Round 1's profile attempts fall on \PDays{} days and round 2's on \TwoDays, so the bootstrap
over days has few to draw from and its intervals are rough. A half of round 2 is \TwoHOneDays{} or
\TwoHTwoDays{} days.
\item Round 2's puzzles were chosen by one of the estimates it tests: the sets aimed from the
view, so most of the view's chances sit between 70\% and 90\%, and both estimates are scored only
on the difficulties the view chose.
\item The legacy log cannot say which attempts were retries; the replay counts them as the app does,
at half weight in the headline and in full in the view. Round 2 scores only attempts the app
recorded as first exposures.
\item A puzzle's published Lichess rating stands for its difficulty, as in the app. An item-level
model would need more than one attempt per puzzle.
\item Round 1's record was rebuilt from memory that selects on the result. Round 2 reads only the
app's own log, written as the attempts are played.
\end{itemize}
