\author{Chess Coach research lab}

\begin{abstract}
At each key moment of a game review, the coaching app explains what the move played allowed,
what the engine's better move does instead, and the difference between them. We measured
those explanations on the \Moments{} key moments of \Games{} of the player's games, and the
later rounds also on endgame sets of \EndgameGames{} and \EndgameTwoGames{} more games, with three
grades checked against the board, and changed the code that writes them one idea per round,
keeping a round only when no grade fell on either half of the games (one round fixed five
text defects at once). Where a change wrote
something the grades cannot check, a judge model compared the old and new texts blind, twice,
with the order reversed. Over \RoundCount{} rounds (\KeptRoundCount{} kept), the share of held-out moments whose
texts make no false checkable claim rose from \BaseTestFaithful\% to \KeptTestFaithful\%, and
the share that names a concrete consequence of the better move from \BaseTestConcrete\% to
\KeptTestConcrete\%. The judge preferred the texts of every kept round it read in most of the
comparisons it decided on each half, and a length control showed that was not for their length: filler as long as a
round's change won \LengthPaddingDecoy{} of \LengthPaddingN{} comparisons against the same text
without it. Each kept round is a change to the app's own code. The lab recomputes every
published number from the records, and the app rerun on fresh engine evidence, carrying the kept
rounds through round \AppKeptThrough, scores \RerunTestFaithful\% and \RerunTestConcrete\% on
the same two measures.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the player reads}

When a game review stops at a key moment (a mistake, a blunder, a missed win), the app shows a
card with a headline and a verdict, a lesson panel (what the move played allowed, what the
better move does instead, the difference between the two lines, a takeaway), a Why sheet with
the evidence behind the verdict, and a retry: the player may try the position again, with a
hint and then the answer. Every one of those texts is written by heuristics in the app's review
and knowledge services from the engine's evidence. No language model writes them, so every
claim they make can be traced to a template, and most can be checked on the board.

The player is an adult club player, rated \PlayerRatingMin--\PlayerRatingMax{} on chess.com across the
\Games{} games studied here. Two things matter: that the texts are true, and that they say
\emph{why} the better move is better, in terms the player can use at the board, not only by how
much.

\section{Data and measures}

\paragraph{Moments.} The \Moments{} key moments the review stops at in \Games{} of the player's
games, from the app's phone-profile engine evidence for each game. The games are split
\GamesTrain{} for train and \GamesTest{} for test, the same split as the accuracy project, so no
game is train in one and test in the other: \MomentsTrain{} train moments and \MomentsTest{}
test moments. A round was designed on train moments only.

\paragraph{Grades.} Each moment's texts get three grades, all checked against the board with
chess.js and against the stored engine lines (\texttt{grade.mjs}):

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{faithful}: 1 unless a checkable claim in the visible text is false: an illegal
move, a line that does not replay, a piece said to attack what it does not, material said to
be won that the line does not win, a mate that is not there, or an ``if they take it with''
answer that is not the engine's line or whose claim does not hold at its end.
\item \textbf{concrete}: 1 when the better move's explanation names a consequence (material, a
mate, a named threat, a prevented reply) and the text says what the move played allowed or
missed.
\item \textbf{named\_deep}: 1 when every move the text names as best rests on a search at the
app's best budget. It is a property of the evidence, not of the words, and is held, not
targeted.
\end{itemize}

A fourth grade, \textbf{faithfulAll}, is faithful over the lesson the Why sheet also shows on a
moment whose card shows only a comment. A clause no template recognises is reported as
unparsed, never graded true. The grader was checked by hand on \HandcheckMoments{} moments
before any round; the hand verdicts and the baseline's grades agree on all three grades for
\HandcheckAgree{} of them.

Every rate below is a share of key moments, with a 95\% interval from a bootstrap over games
(a game's moments share its evidence, so the game is the unit). A variant's difference from the
baseline is paired on the same moments.

\paragraph{The judge.} The grades cannot see whether a new clause \emph{helps}. For a round that
writes something new, every moment whose texts change is shown to a judge model as two versions
(X and Y, by a seeded coin), each laid out as the app lays it out, with the position, the move,
the verdict, the engine's evaluation and best line. The judge answers which version would help
the player more (understand why the better move is better, and know what to look for next
time), or that they are the same, with one sentence of reason. It answers twice, with X and Y
swapped the second time. The rule, fixed before any run: keep the round if, pooling both runs,
the new texts are preferred in more than half of the decided comparisons on train \emph{and} on
test.

\section{Is the judge rewarding length?}
\label{sec:length}

Before round 10, the judge picked the longer of its two versions in \LongerPicked{} of the
\LongerDecided{} comparisons it decided in rounds \LongerRounds. A judge that simply prefers
longer text would have voted the same way, so a length check, fixed before any of its answers,
tested that on the moments of rounds \LengthRounds. Each moment gave three comparisons: the
longer text against itself; a \emph{decoy}, the shorter text with filler about as long as the
round's change, against the shorter text; and the longer text against the decoy. The filler
either restates what the card already shows (its line, or that the move is the engine's first
choice) or is advice that fits any move. The last two comparisons were shown in both orders, and
the \LengthItems{} items were dealt into \LengthBatches{} batches under opaque ids, each read by
a fresh judge with the rounds' instructions, so no judge could tell a control from a round.

The judge answered ``same'' to \LengthIdentitySame{} of \LengthIdentityN{} identical pairs, and
preferred the decoy in \LengthPaddingDecoy{} of \LengthPaddingN{} comparisons with the shorter
text (Table~\ref{tab:controls}). The rounds that added words won on what the words said.
From round 10 on, each round's batches also carry a padding control: moments the round does not
change, set against themselves with filler as long as the round's change. If the decoy wins at
least a quarter of those comparisons and more than half of the decided ones, the judge has drifted
toward length and the round is not kept on its word. In round 10 the decoy won \PadTenDecoy{} of
\PadTenN, and in round 12 \PadTwelveDecoy{} of \PadTwelveN. Round 13 set out to shorten its texts, so
its batches carry the mirror, a trim control: moments the round does not change, set against themselves
with the last point of the better move's section cut. A judge drifting toward short texts would
prefer the cut one, under the same rule; it did in \TrimThirteenDecoy{} of \TrimThirteenN{}
comparisons (\TrimThirteenUnchanged{} the full text, \TrimThirteenSame{} same). Round 15 grows the
section on what the played move allowed, so its padding control pads that section, on moments where
it says nothing and the round leaves it so; the decoy won \PadFifteenDecoy{} of \PadFifteenN{}
comparisons (\PadFifteenUnchanged{} the shorter text, \PadFifteenSame{} same). Round 16 grows
several sections of the card at once, so its padding control adds a sentence of filler after the
difference, as long as the round's median growth of the whole card, on endgame moments the round
leaves unchanged; the decoy won \PadSixteenDecoy{} of \PadSixteenN{} comparisons
(\PadSixteenUnchanged{} the shorter text, \PadSixteenSame{} same). Round 17 adds one sentence at
the end of the difference, so its padding control does as round 16's did, on moments of the endgame
set the round leaves unchanged; the decoy won \PadSeventeenDecoy{} of \PadSeventeenN{} comparisons
(\PadSeventeenUnchanged{} the shorter text, \PadSeventeenSame{} same). Round 18 adds one sentence
to the difference, so its padding control does the same; the decoy won \PadEighteenDecoy{} of
\PadEighteenN{} comparisons (\PadEighteenUnchanged{} the shorter text, \PadEighteenSame{} same).
Rounds 19, 20b, 21 and 22 each add one sentence to the difference as well, and their padding
controls do as round 18's did: the decoy won \PadNineteenDecoy{} of \PadNineteenN{} comparisons in

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item The grades check what can be checked: a true and concrete text can still fail to teach.
That question goes to the player on the Calibration page, and to the judge, whose agreement with
the player is itself only partly measured.
\item The length check shows that the judge does not reward words that say nothing. It does not
show that the judge can tell a point that helps from a true point that does not, at the same
length; a decoy built from true but beside-the-point facts about the position would test that.
\item The judge is a model reading on the player's behalf. It is a good finder of what is
missing and a poorer judge of how much it matters, which is why the rounds use its pairwise
preferences and its notes, not its ratings.
\item Round 12 answers one defense, the model's likeliest; when the defender's second choice is
nearly as likely, the lesson leaves it unanswered.
\item Round 16 names a king's walk in one line without asking whether the other line makes it too;
where both do, the point does not tell the moves apart. What separates such moves is often a king
cut off along a file, which no reading names yet.
\item Round 17's judges read a sample of the moments it changed, \RoundSeventeenSampled{} of
\RoundSeventeenChanged, so its verdict is the sample's. Its sentence that both moves leave the mover
ahead or behind is the one the judges did not always prefer.
\item Round 18 names the square a check takes from the king without asking whether another of the
mover's pieces already covers it; on \RoundEighteenHeldNothing{} of its sentences one does, so the
king's guard there held nothing, and the one answer the judges gave against the round was on such
a sentence.
\item Round 19 names the guard a played move gives up without asking whether the better move gives
it up too; on \RoundNineteenBothLeave{} of its \RoundNineteenJudged{} judged sentences nothing of
the mover's guards the square after the better move either, and the one answer the judges gave
against the round was on such a sentence.
\item Round 20b calls the attacked piece's move an escape even where the mover can take it at once
on its new square, a sacrifice rather than an escape: \RoundTwentyHangs{} of its
