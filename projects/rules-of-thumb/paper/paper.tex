\begin{abstract}
A human coach explains a middlegame or endgame move by a rule of thumb: walk the king toward the
pawns, put the rook behind the passed pawn, take the open file. The app teaches fifteen such rules,
and its board code says when a move shows one. In rated 10-minute Lichess games between players at
his level and the band above, the move a rule points to, as a player at that level would play it,
is sound less often than the move such a player would naturally play, for all \Judged{} rules with
enough positions, on the train months and on the held-out month: by \LeastLift{} points for
\LeastLiftName{} up to \MostLift{} for \MostLiftName. That verdict, fixed before the run, cannot by
itself tell a rule from a random choice, so a control added after it draws in each position as many
random legal moves as the rule had and keeps the one Maia-2 likes best. That move trails the natural
move too, by \NullLow{} to \NullHigh{} points, and against it \NBetter{} rules point to better
moves than chance on both periods; only \NoDiffName{} does not. The rules carry information, then,
but following one where it differs from the natural move costs \CondLow{} to \CondHigh{} points. In
\ExN{} held-out examples, \ExCaught{} of the \ExUnsound{} unsound rule moves allow mate or lose
material by the app's own blunder check, and \ExForcing{} more pass over a check, a capture or a
promotion that the natural move took. A rule of thumb is a reason to look at a move once the checks,
captures and threats are seen, not a reason to play it.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question}

A coach explains a move by a rule of thumb more often than by a line: the king is a fighting piece
once the queens are off, a rook belongs behind a passed pawn, a knight is strongest on a square no
pawn can attack. The app teaches these rules as concepts, and its move detectors say when a move
shows one. That is the kind of understanding a player can carry from one game to the next, so it is
worth knowing which rules hold at his level.

In the middlegames and endgames that players at his level reach, how often is the move a rule points
to sound, is it sounder than the move a player at his level would naturally play, and where does the
rule fail? A rule that holds is one the app can teach as a way to choose a move. One that does no
better than the natural move is not worth a lesson for that, and one that does worse misleads.

\section{How it was measured}

\paragraph{The games.} Rated standard Lichess games at 10 minutes with no increment, his time
control, \GamesMonth{} a month in file order: \TrainMonths{} to train on and \HeldMonth{} held out,
\GamesAll{} games with \PositionsAll{} middlegame and endgame positions in all, the phase read by
the app's divider. A game enters when both players are in his band or the band above: his band is
\HisLow{} to \HisHigh{} on Lichess, around his chess.com rapid rating by the app's offset, and the
band above \AboveLow{} to \AboveHigh. On Maia-2's scale they read \MaiaHis{} and \MaiaAbove.

\paragraph{The rules.} Fifteen, each read by the app's own code at commit \texttt{\AppCommit}. A
move shows a rule when the app's move description holds a finding of that concept for the mover
with the tone of a gain. The outside passed pawn, the Lucena position and the Philidor position
are only ever written as notes, so their notes count, each for the side the position is for;
trading when ahead is read from its technique reader, which the move description could not reach
(a gap reported to the app and since fixed); and the worst piece is read from the app's activity
reading: a move by the piece the app names the mover's worst, after which it has more legal moves.
Tactics and mates are left out: they are calculation, which the puzzles train.

\paragraph{The positions.} For each rule, a game gives at most one position, drawn at random among
its positions where the rule is available (some legal move shows it), up to \Cap{} a month for
each rule: \Scored{} positions in all.

\paragraph{The moves compared.} In each position, $R$ is the rule's move: among the legal moves
that show the rule, the one Maia-2 rates most likely at the mover's band, the rule as a player at
that band would play it. $N$, the natural move, is Maia-2's most likely move of all, and $A$ the
move played.

\paragraph{The referee.} Stockfish through the app's engine service at its grading budget of
\GradeNodes{} nodes, the hash cleared before each position: the best is searched with one line, and
each move is scored at the same budget. A move is \emph{sound} when it gives up no more than
\Window{} win\% against the best. With seven men or fewer the app's tablebase probe decides: a move
is sound when it keeps the mover's outcome.

\paragraph{The rule, fixed before any position was scored.} The \emph{lift} is $R$'s soundness
minus $N$'s, paired by position, with 95\% intervals from \Draws{} bootstrap draws over games (seed
\Seed). A rule is taught when the lift's interval lies above 0 on the train months and on the
held-out month; it misleads when it lies below 0 on both; it is no better than the natural move
otherwise; and under \MinHeld{} held-out positions there is not enough data. Reported beside the
verdict, a rule is a \emph{step up} when the band above plays $R$ more often than his band, the
difference's interval above 0 on both periods. A split of the positions, read before the move, is a
\emph{failure condition} when in one of its cells $R$ is sound at least 10 points less often than
in the rest, with the difference's interval below 0, on both periods.

\section{The verdicts: every rule misleads}

Every rule with enough data misleads (Table~\ref{tab:verdicts}). On the held-out month the rule's
move is sound less often than the natural move by \LeastLift{} points for \LeastLiftName{} up to
\MostLift{} for \MostLiftName, and the train months give the same verdicts. The rule's move is
sound less often than the move actually played as well, for every rule. The Lucena and Philidor
positions came up \LucenaHeld{} and \PhilidorHeld{} times in the held-out month, too few for a
verdict.

Only \StepUpName{} is a step up: players in the band above play its move \StepUpGap{} points more
often than players in his band (\StepUpGapCi{} on the held-out month). How findable the rules' moves
are varies widely: at his band Maia-2 gives the rule's move \FindLow\% on average for the rarest and
\FindHigh\% for the most common.

\section{A rule with no information}

The run's review found that the lift's zero is not a neutral rule's. $R$ is the move Maia-2 likes
best among the moves that show the rule and $N$ the one it likes best of all, so $R$ is $N$ wherever
$N$ shows the rule, and wherever $R$ differs, Maia-2 rated it lower. Any subset of the legal moves
would trail $N$, or at best tie it, by this measure. So a control, written down before it was computed, draws in each
scored position as many legal moves at random as the rule had, without replacement and seeded by
the position, and keeps the one Maia-2 likes best, $R_0$, refereed as $R$ was. The \emph{random
rule's lift}, $R_0$'s soundness minus $N$'s, is what a rule with no information shows; the
\emph{rule's content}, $R$'s minus $R_0$'s, is what the rule adds to chance. It changes no verdict.

The random rule misleads too, and by more: its held-out lift lies \NullLow{} to \NullHigh{} points
below 0 (Table~\ref{tab:control}). Against it, \NBetter{} rules point to better moves than chance on
both periods, from \ContentLow{} points for \ContentLowName{} to \ContentHigh{} for
\ContentHighName. Only \NoDiffName{} reads no differently from chance: its content is
\NoDiffContentTrain{} points (\NoDiffContentTrainCi) on the train months and \NoDiffContent{}
(\NoDiffContentCi) held out. The random moves took \FreshSearches{} fresh Stockfish searches, and
each found the best the run had stored.

So the rules know something: the moves they point to are sound more often than moves drawn at
random in the same number. But wherever the rule's move is not the natural move it is sound less
often, by \CondLow{} points for \CondLowName{} up to \CondHigh{} for \CondHighName{} on the held-out
month, for every rule on both periods. How often the two agree varies just as widely, from \SameLow\%
of the held-out positions for \SameLowName{} to \SameHigh\% for \SameHighName.

\section{Where the rules fail}

The failure conditions were fixed with the rule (Table~\ref{tab:failures}). The rule's move fails
when it is quiet for \FailQuiet{} rules, when the material is level for \FailLevel, when the
opponent has a threat (a mate in one or a winning capture, by the app's reading of the position)
for \FailThreat, in the middlegame for \FailMiddle, and when the mover's king shows a danger sign
for \FailDanger. With a threat on the board the gap is \ThreatGapLow{} to \ThreatGapHigh{} points:
the active king's move is sound \AkThreat\% of the time when the opponent threatens something and
\AkNoThreat\% when not. No rule fails where there is no threat or where its move is a capture or a
check, and \NoFailNames{} have no failure condition at all.

Level material is probably in part the referee's doing, an inference this study does not test: a
fixed window in win\% is narrower in centipawns where the game is balanced than where it is won or
lost, so fewer moves pass it.

\section{What the examples show}

The control added \ExPer{} held-out positions for each judged rule, drawn at random among those
where the rule's move is not the natural move: \ExN{} in all, \ExStockfish{} refereed by Stockfish
and \ExTablebase{} by the tablebase (\path{results/examples.md}). After the results, each was read
with the app's own blunder check, the reading its live coach makes before it warns about a move
(Table~\ref{tab:examples}, \path{results/why.md}): whether a move allows mate in one, and whether it
loses material, either as a capture the capture war says loses or by leaving a piece that can be
taken for a gain, newly loose or loose already. The capturing piece of an even trade is not counted,
since taking it back is the trade.

Of the \ExUnsound{} rule moves the referee found unsound, the check flags \ExCaught: \ExMate{} allow
mate and \ExLoses{} lose material. Of the rest, \ExForcing{} pass over a check, a capture or a
promotion that the natural move took, and only \ExQuiet{} were a quieter choice that was simply
worse. The check flags \ExSoundFlagged{} of the \ExSound{} sound rule moves too, and the natural move
\ExNFlagged{} times in \ExN. The natural move is a check, a capture or a promotion in \ExNForcing{}
of the examples, the rule's move in \ExRForcing. Figure~\ref{fig:kinds} shows one rule move of each
of the first three kinds, chosen to show them; every example is in the results.

\section{Do the detectors read too widely?}

The study tests each rule as the app's board code reads it, so a misleading verdict may be the
detector's fault. In the examples most detectors read their rule as it is taught: the king walks
toward the pawns, the rook goes behind a passed pawn or to the seventh rank, a knight or bishop lands
on a square no pawn can attack, a pawn strikes a chain. What fails is not the reading but the
moment, as above. Two read wider than a coach would (Figure~\ref{fig:wide}).

\paragraph{The outside passed pawn} is read from a note the app writes on any move after which the
mover has a new passed pawn far from the other pawns and from their king. The note does not ask what
the move gave for it. All \OpCaptures{} of its examples' rule moves are captures, and \OpLosing{} of
them lose material by the capture war, each a rook for a pawn (Figure~\ref{fig:wide}a). Its verdict is in part the reading's, though its moves are still sound far
more often than random ones. The app wrote the same note on such a move in Game Review, beside the
warning that the rook is lost; since chess PR 166, after the commit measured here, the review holds a
technique note back on a move that gives its piece away.

\paragraph{Cutting the king off} is read on any rook move, in a rook ending, to a file with nothing
else on it between their king and one of the mover's passed pawns. It does not ask whether their
king could reach the pawn without the cut, and it is the one rule whose moves are no better than
chance (Figure~\ref{fig:wide}b).

\paragraph{The worst piece and the open file} are each available in most positions (\WorstAvail\%
and \OpenAvail\% of the held-out ones): nearly any rook move to a file without the mover's pawns
shows the open file, and the worst piece, the lab's own reading rather than the app's, takes any move
that gives the least mobile piece more moves. Such a rule rarely says when; the worst piece carries
the least content of the twelve.

\section{What it means for the app}

A rule of thumb is a reason to look at a move and a way to explain one; at his level it is not a way
to choose one. The rules point to better moves than chance, so they belong in the app's teaching.
But the move a rule points to, played as his level would play it, is sound less often than the move
his level plays naturally, and where the two differ the rule's move mostly ignores something
forcing: a piece left loose, a mate in one allowed, a check, capture or promotion passed over. The
app's own blunder check catches most of these.

The app already uses its rules that way in Game Review: it praises a rule only on a move that gave
up less than its praise bar, and names a rule the player missed only on Stockfish's best move. Its
pawn-break lesson now says to check what a break leaves hanging before playing it (chess PR 130,
after the commit measured here), and its worst-piece lesson begins ``No tactics?''. Its open-file
and outpost lessons taught their rule with no such check, and the evidence here says they should
start from it; since chess PR 165 they do, looking at every check, capture and threat first and
treating the open file or the hole as where to look once those are answered.

Two readings were for the review to weigh. The outside passed pawn's note was a fact about the board
written whatever the move cost, so it appeared beside a blunder as if it were the move's reason; the
review now holds it back on a move that gives its piece away (chess PR 166). And the cut-off reading
carries no information at this level; the app's endgame drill for it is a
position where the cut is the answer, which this study does not test.

The next round, rules-after-check, tested the coach's advice as a coach gives it: the rule's move
among the moves the blunder check passes, in positions with no tactic on the board, against the
natural move among the same moves. Its control failed, so it gives no verdict, but there too the
rule's move is sound less often than the natural move, for every rule with enough positions.

\section{Caveats}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Not his games.} The positions are Lichess players' in his band and the band above, by
  the app's conversion of his rating; his own games are too few for most rules.
\item \textbf{One move for a plan.} A rule is tested as the next move his level would play for it,
  not as a plan over several moves.
\item \textbf{Sound is not instructive.} A sound move can be the second-best plan; the lift compares
  the rule's move only with the natural move.
\item \textbf{A strong baseline.} Maia-2's most likely move at the mover's band is sound more often
  than the move actually played, for every rule (Table~\ref{tab:verdicts}).
\item \textbf{Added afterwards.} The control, the examples and their reading by the blunder check
  were added after the rule was applied. They describe and change no verdict, and the examples are
  few, \ExPer{} a rule.
\end{itemize}
